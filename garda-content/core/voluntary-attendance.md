A Garda has asked you to come into the station. Perhaps it was a phone call, perhaps a card through the door. You have not been arrested, nobody has used the word suspect, and it has been described as a chat, or making a statement, or clearing something up. This is one of the most important moments to take advice, precisely because it does not feel like one. Ring 087 122 3080 before you agree to a time.

> **If the Gardai are waiting**
> You do not have to answer questions at the door or on the phone. Say that you will take legal advice and come back to them, then ring 087 122 3080.

## Why you are being asked

Investigations do not begin with arrests. They begin with accounts, and an interview by appointment is often the most efficient way of gathering one. There are several reasons you might be asked in.

- You witnessed something and your account is wanted.
- You are the injured party and a statement is being taken.
- Your name has come up and the Gardai want to hear what you say about it.
- There is a suspicion about you, and an arrest is not thought necessary.

You will not always be told which of these you are. That uncertainty is the reason for taking advice, not a reason to skip it.

## What "voluntary" actually means

Voluntary describes how you got there. It does not change what happens once you are in the interview room.

- **What you say is used in the same way.** A voluntary interview is ordinarily recorded, and an account given at an appointment can be relied on in court just as one given in custody can.
- **A caution can be administered.** If you are cautioned, you are being treated as a suspect, whatever word was used on the phone. That is a moment to stop and take advice.
- **You are still entitled to legal advice,** and in the ordinary course to have a solicitor present.
- **You may be free to leave.** A person attending voluntarily is ordinarily not in custody, and if that changes you should be told.
- **An arrest can still happen at the station.** Attending by arrangement does not prevent one.

## Going in as a witness, coming out as a suspect

This is the situation that causes the most harm, and it is not rare.

People attend to be helpful. In giving an account they explain where they were, who they were with, what they did. Somewhere in that account is a detail that alters how the matter is viewed, and the questions turn. A caution follows. By then, forty minutes of unadvised answers already exist on a recording and they cannot be unsaid.

None of that means you are being tricked. It means an interview is an evidence gathering exercise. If your account helps you, it will still help you after you have taken advice. If part of it exposes you, you need to know that before you speak.

## What to do before you agree to attend

1. **Do not agree to a time on the spot.** Say you will confirm shortly. That is a normal and unremarkable response.
2. **Write down exactly what you were told**: the Garda's name, the station, the number, the date and time of contact, and the words used about why you are wanted.
3. **Ring a solicitor before you commit.** 087 122 3080, and repeat what you were told, word for word if you can.
4. **Let your solicitor contact the station.** We can ask what the interview concerns, whether you are being treated as a witness or a suspect, and whether you will be cautioned. Those answers change the advice completely.
5. **Arrange the appointment through your solicitor,** so it is scheduled for a time when a solicitor can be with you.
6. **Say nothing about the substance in the meantime,** and hand nothing over without advice.

## Should you attend at all

There is no single answer, and anyone who gives you one without knowing the circumstances is guessing.

Sometimes attending with a solicitor present is sensible, and a clear account given early resolves a matter. Sometimes the safer course is to decline the interview, or to provide a prepared written statement instead. It depends on what is alleged, what evidence exists, whether you have an account that assists you, and what the consequences of not attending might be.

Declining is not always the end of it. The Gardai may proceed differently, and other powers may be available. That is a factor to weigh, not a reason to attend without advice.

## If you decide to go

- **Have a solicitor present,** rather than simply having spoken to one beforehand.
- **Ask at the start whether you are free to leave** and whether you are being cautioned, and ask for the answers to be recorded.
- **Ask what the interview concerns** before it begins.
- **Do not guess at detail.** "I do not recall" is an honest answer and a safe one.
- **Ask for a break** if something new is put to you, and take advice before responding.
- **Read anything before you sign it,** and do not sign a statement you disagree with.

## Common questions

### Can I be arrested if I go in voluntarily?

Yes. Attending by arrangement does not prevent an arrest, and a voluntary attendance can become a detention. That is one reason to involve a solicitor before you go rather than after.

### Do I have to go?

It depends on the circumstances and on what powers may be available, so it is not a question to answer from a web page. Take advice on 087 122 3080 before you agree or refuse. A solicitor can also make the contact with the station for you.

### They said I am only a witness. Is that not fine?

It may well be, and many witness statements are entirely straightforward. But the description you are given at the outset is not a guarantee of how the interview will develop. A short call before you attend settles the point.

### Is a voluntary interview covered by free legal advice?

The Garda Station Legal Advice Scheme is means tested and administered by the Legal Aid Board, and how it applies depends on your circumstances and on the basis for the interview. Ask us when you ring. See [legal aid](/legal-aid).

### I have already been interviewed without a solicitor. Is it too late?

Ring anyway. What was said cannot be withdrawn, but how the matter proceeds from here is still open, and further interviews or a file to the Director of Public Prosecutions may follow.
