Being arrested is disorientating, and most people going through it have never been in a Garda station before. It helps to know the shape of what is coming, because very little of it is decided in the first five minutes and almost none of it is improved by talking. If an interview has not yet started, stop and ring 087 122 3080.

> **If the Gardai are waiting**
> Tell the member in charge you are not answering questions until you have had legal advice, and that you are nominating your own solicitor. Then ring 087 122 3080.

## Arriving at the station and the custody record

When you are brought in you are taken to the member in charge, the Garda responsible for the welfare of people in custody there. You should be told what you have been arrested for and why you are being detained.

A custody record is opened. It logs your arrival, the reason for arrest, everything taken from you, every period of questioning, meals, rest, medical attention and each request you make, including a request for a solicitor. It can matter a great deal later. This is the point to say what the station needs to know about you.

1. **Ask for a solicitor** and give a name and number if you have one. Ours is 087 122 3080.
2. **Ask that a named person be notified** of where you are.
3. **Declare any medical need**: medication, a condition, an injury, pregnancy, withdrawal, a mental health difficulty.
4. **Ask for an interpreter** if English is not the language you are comfortable being questioned in.
5. **Say if you are under 18** and ask that your parent or guardian be contacted.
6. **Say nothing about the allegation itself** while doing any of the above.

## Searches, property and samples

You will ordinarily be searched and your property taken and listed. Read the property list before signing it and say if anything is missing or wrongly described.

Photographs, fingerprints and other samples may be taken during a detention. The rules differ depending on what is sought: some things can be taken without your agreement, some require consent, and refusing can itself carry consequences in certain circumstances. Before you consent to anything, or refuse it, ask to speak to your solicitor. The same applies to a request to unlock a phone.

## The interview

Interviews are ordinarily recorded. You may be interviewed more than once, and the tone can change considerably between sessions. Before it begins you are entitled to legal advice and, in the ordinary course, to have a solicitor present. Ask for both.

- **Nothing is off the record.** Remarks in a corridor, in a cell, or while an interview is being set up are still noted.
- **Silence is a real option, but it has limits.** In certain limited circumstances a court may be permitted to draw inferences from a failure to answer particular questions. Whether that risk arises depends on the circumstances, which is why the decision needs advice first.
- **There are options other than talk or silence,** including a prepared written statement, or answering some matters and not others.
- **Do not guess.** An honest "I do not remember" is safer than a helpful estimate that proves wrong.
- **Ask for a pause** if something new is put to you, and take advice before answering.
- **Do not sign anything you have not read,** including notes of interview.

## How long you can be held

Detention exists so that an investigation can be carried out, not indefinitely. The maximum period depends on the legislation you are held under, and different powers carry very different periods. In some cases a period can be extended by a senior officer, or on application to a court.

Time can also be suspended, for rest overnight or for medical attention, so elapsed time is not always the detention time being counted. Your solicitor can establish which power you are held under and what it means for you.

## How detention ends

There are four common outcomes, and they are not equally serious.

1. **Released without charge, no further action.** The investigation is at an end so far as you are concerned.
2. **Released without charge while a file goes to the Director of Public Prosecutions.** Nothing is decided yet. A decision may take weeks or months. This is common, and it is not the same as being cleared.
3. **Charged and released on station bail.** You get a charge sheet with a date, a court and conditions. Read the conditions carefully. Breaching them, including failing to appear, is a separate offence.
4. **Charged and brought before the next sitting of the District Court.** If the court is not sitting, that may mean a special sitting, including at a weekend. See [nights, weekends and bank holidays](/weekend-out-of-hours).

You may also be released and asked to return voluntarily later. Take advice before agreeing: see [attending voluntarily](/voluntary-attendance).

## What happens next

A first court appearance is usually short and administrative: the charge is put, bail is considered, and a date is fixed. It is not the trial, and nothing is decided about guilt that day.

Between now and then: keep every document you were given, write down what happened while it is fresh and give that note to your solicitor, do not contact the complainant or any witness whether directly or through somebody else, stay off social media about the matter, and delete nothing.

Tell your solicitor the awkward parts too. They are usually what a defence has to be built around, and they are far less damaging when they arrive early.

## Common questions

### Does being arrested mean I have a criminal record?

No. An arrest is not a conviction and it is not a charge. Many arrests end without any charge at all. If you are worried about employment, vetting or travel, raise it with your solicitor.

### They said it will be quicker if I just explain. Is that true?

Explaining does not shorten a detention period, and an account given without advice cannot be withdrawn later. If you have something to say, there are safer ways of saying it.

### I have been released while a file goes to the DPP. What now?

Keep your solicitor instructed rather than assuming it has gone away, and preserve anything that might help. If you are contacted again, ring 087 122 3080 before you attend or answer questions.

### Who pays for a solicitor at the station and in court?

Advice at the station is covered for most people by the Garda Station Legal Advice Scheme, which is means tested and administered by the Legal Aid Board. Criminal legal aid in court is separate. See [legal aid](/legal-aid).
