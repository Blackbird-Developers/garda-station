If your case has been sent forward on indictment, it has left the District Court and will be tried before a judge and a jury. That is usually the point at which a case starts to feel serious in a different way, and at which the preparation changes completely.

In Dublin this court sits at the [Criminal Courts of Justice](/criminal-courts-of-justice) on Parkgate Street. Outside Dublin it sits in the courthouse for the relevant circuit.

## What "sent forward" actually means

It means the Director of Public Prosecutions has directed that the case be tried on indictment rather than summarily, and the [District Court](/dublin-district-court) has sent you forward to a higher court for trial.

Before that happens you are served with a book of evidence: the prosecution's case in writing, with the statements of the witnesses it intends to call and the exhibits. It is usually the first time you see the case against you in full, and going through it with your solicitor is one of the most important things you will do.

This court tries most indictable offences. The most serious categories are reserved for [the Central Criminal Court](/central-criminal-court).

## How it differs from the District Court

- **A jury decides the facts.** Twelve members of the public hear the evidence and return the verdict. The judge rules on the law and, on a conviction, decides sentence.
- **Barristers appear.** The prosecution is conducted by counsel instructed by the Director of Public Prosecutions, and the defence normally briefs counsel.
- **The case is prepared in writing first.** Disclosure and applications about what the jury may hear are dealt with before or during the trial.
- **The timescale is longer,** measured in months rather than weeks, and trial dates can move.
- **A trial takes days,** and you must attend every day of it.

## From send forward to trial

1. The book of evidence is served and you are sent forward for trial.
2. The case is listed and given a date for arraignment.
3. At arraignment the indictment is put to you and you plead to each count.
4. If you plead not guilty, a trial date is fixed and pre-trial matters are dealt with on mention dates.
5. Further disclosure is sought and any expert or defence evidence is prepared.
6. The trial runs: jury selection, prosecution evidence, the defence case if one is called, closing speeches, the judge's charge, then the verdict.
7. On a conviction or a plea, sentencing is usually adjourned.

## What the wait is like

You will normally be on bail while you wait, on the terms the court has set, and you are expected to keep to them without exception. The waiting is the hardest part for most clients, and it is worth using rather than sitting in: employment, treatment and courses all matter later.

On trial days you sit in the body of the court, not the witness box, unless and until you give evidence. Whether you do is decided with advice.

## Common questions

### Do I have to have a jury trial?

No. You can plead guilty at arraignment, in which case the case moves to sentencing and no jury is empanelled. That decision should only follow going through the book of evidence.

### How long will it take to come on?

It varies with the court's list, the complexity of the case and whether disclosure is outstanding. Dates can move.

### Will I be in custody in the meantime?

Not necessarily. Many people are on bail throughout. Whether bail continues after send forward depends on the case and on how you have kept to your conditions.

### What happens if the jury cannot agree?

The judge may discharge the jury. The prosecution then decides whether to seek a retrial, and your solicitor will explain where that leaves you.
