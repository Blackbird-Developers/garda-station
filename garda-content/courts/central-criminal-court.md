The Central Criminal Court is the High Court sitting in its criminal jurisdiction. It tries the most serious offences in Irish law, and if a case has been sent there, it is as serious as a criminal charge gets.

In Dublin it sits at the [Criminal Courts of Justice](/criminal-courts-of-justice) on Parkgate Street, and it also sits outside Dublin from time to time.

## What is tried there

Only a defined group of offences goes to this court. In broad terms it deals with murder and attempted murder, rape and the most serious sexual offences, and a small number of other offences the law reserves to it. Everything else on indictment goes to [the Circuit Criminal Court](/circuit-criminal-court).

A case arrives the same way as any indictable matter. The [District Court](/dublin-district-court) sends the accused forward once the book of evidence has been served, and the case is then listed for arraignment and, if the plea is not guilty, for trial.

## Judge and jury

The trial is heard by a High Court judge sitting with a jury of twelve, and the division of work between them is strict.

The jury decides the facts: what happened, whose account it accepts, and whether the prosecution has proved the case beyond reasonable doubt. The judge decides questions of law, rules on what evidence the jury may hear, directs the jury on the law, and on a conviction deals with sentence. For a small number of offences the sentence is fixed by law rather than left to the judge.

A jury is asked first to try to reach a unanimous verdict, and in certain circumstances the judge may accept a verdict on which not every juror agrees. If no verdict can be reached, the jury may be discharged and the prosecution decides whether to seek a retrial.

## What the process involves for you

1. **The book of evidence.** You and your solicitor go through the prosecution case statement by statement. This is where the defence starts.
2. **Arraignment.** The counts on the indictment are put to you in open court and you plead to each one.
3. **Preparation.** Disclosure is sought, expert evidence considered and issues about admissibility identified. Most of this is never visible to anyone but your legal team.
4. **The trial.** Legal argument is often heard before the jury is empanelled. Then jury selection, prosecution evidence, any defence evidence, closing speeches, the judge's charge, and the verdict. It can run for days or weeks.
5. **Sentencing.** On a conviction or a plea this is usually adjourned, for reports and for any victim impact evidence.

## The practical realities

Cases here take a long time to reach trial, and you may be in custody or on strict bail conditions meanwhile. Trials are often reported by the press, which is hard on families and worth preparing for. You are entitled to counsel, ordinarily senior and junior, instructed by your solicitor, and to legal aid where you qualify.

## Common questions

### Why is my case here and not in the Circuit Court?

Because the offence charged falls within the group the law reserves to this court. It is a question of the charge, not of anything anyone has chosen.

### Will my name be in the papers?

Trials are held in public and are often reported. Reporting is restricted or anonymity applies in some situations, particularly in certain sexual offence cases.

### Will I have to give evidence?

Not necessarily. You cannot be compelled to give evidence in your own trial, and whether you should is decided with advice once the prosecution case has gone in.

### How long before it comes on, and do I attend every day?

Timing varies, because these cases involve extensive disclosure. Once a trial starts you must attend every day it sits.
