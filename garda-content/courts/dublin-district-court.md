Almost every criminal case in Ireland starts in the District Court, whatever it turns into later. If you have been charged, this is the court you will see first, and often the only one you will ever see.

The Dublin Metropolitan District Court sits at the [Criminal Courts of Justice](/criminal-courts-of-justice) on Parkgate Street, Dublin 7. Some District Court business for the county is heard outside the city centre, so check the address on your summons. The wrong courthouse counts as not turning up at all.

## What this court deals with

A District Court judge sits alone. There is no jury, so the judge decides both what happened and what the law requires.

The court hears summary matters: road traffic offences, public order matters, minor theft, criminal damage, possession of drugs for personal use and less serious assaults. Some offences can go either way, and whether this court keeps such a case or sends it higher depends on the view the judge takes of the facts, on the directions of the Director of Public Prosecutions, and sometimes on your own consent.

Much of its work is not a trial at all: first appearances, bail, remands, legal aid, disclosure, and sending cases forward on indictment to [the Circuit Criminal Court](/circuit-criminal-court).

## A first appearance after charge

If you were charged at the station, you were either given a date to attend or brought before the next sitting. Either way the pattern is broadly similar.

1. Your case is called and you come forward. Lists are long, so expect to wait.
2. A Garda gives evidence of arrest, charge and caution: a formal account of how the charge was put to you.
3. Directions from the Director of Public Prosecutions may not have arrived, in which case the case is adjourned.
4. Bail is dealt with, if you are in custody or conditions are sought.
5. Your solicitor applies for legal aid and asks the court to direct disclosure.
6. A further date is fixed and you are remanded to it, on bail or in custody.

Very little is decided on that first morning. That is normal, and it is not a bad sign.

## Bail and remands

Bail is about whether you are released while the case is dealt with, not about guilt. The court can release you on your own bond, ask for an independent surety, or impose conditions: an address to reside at, a curfew, signing on at a Garda station, surrender of a passport, or staying away from named people.

Bail can be opposed, and if it is, the court hears evidence and argument first. What happens depends on the charge, your circumstances and your record, so it needs advice.

Remanded on continuing bail means release on the same terms until the next date. Remanded in custody means you are held until then. Either way, missing the date is serious in itself.

## If the case stays here

Where the matter is dealt with summarily, you either plead guilty or the case is set down for hearing. At a hearing the prosecution calls its witnesses, your solicitor cross examines them, and the defence case is put. The judge then decides, and on a conviction may deal with it that day or adjourn for a report. The outcome depends entirely on the case.

## Common questions

### Do I have to attend every date?

Assume yes unless the court excuses you. Failing to appear can lead to a warrant.

### Will I get legal aid?

Criminal legal aid here is granted by the judge and depends on your means and on what is at stake. Bring proof of income or social welfare payments, and see our [legal aid page](/legal-aid).

### I already have a solicitor. Can I change?

You are entitled to choose who represents you at any stage. Ring us with the charge sheet and the next date.
