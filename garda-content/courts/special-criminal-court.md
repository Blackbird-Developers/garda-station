The Special Criminal Court is a criminal trial court that sits without a jury. Cases before it are heard and decided by three judges sitting together. If a case has been listed there, this page sets out what that means. In Dublin the court sits at the [Criminal Courts of Justice](/criminal-courts-of-justice) on Parkgate Street.

## How the court is made up

The court is composed of three judges, drawn from the existing judiciary and appointed to sit in it. Because there is no jury, those three decide both the law and the facts. They rule on admissibility, assess the credibility of witnesses, and decide whether the prosecution has proved its case beyond reasonable doubt. Where they are not unanimous, the majority view governs.

One practical consequence follows from the absence of a jury. A jury gives a verdict without reasons. This court delivers a reasoned judgment, setting out what it has found and why, and that judgment is a document your legal team can examine closely.

## The categories of case it deals with

Two routes bring a case before the court.

- **Scheduled offences.** Certain categories of offence are scheduled, which in general terms covers offences under legislation concerned with the security of the State and offences connected with unlawful organisations. A charge in a scheduled category ordinarily comes here.
- **Non-scheduled offences certified by the Director of Public Prosecutions.** Where the Director certifies that the ordinary courts are inadequate to secure the effective administration of justice and the preservation of public peace and order, a case can be sent here even though the offence itself is not scheduled. In practice this route is most associated with prosecutions arising from serious organised crime investigations.

Whether either route applies is a question for the prosecution and, ultimately, the courts. It is not something an accused chooses.

## What a case there involves

1. The accused is charged and brought before the court, which deals with remands and bail.
2. A book of evidence is served and the accused is sent forward for trial.
3. The indictment is put and the accused pleads to each count.
4. Disclosure is sought and pre-trial legal issues identified. These cases often involve lengthy investigations, surveillance material, communications evidence and expert analysis.
5. The trial is heard by the three judges: the prosecution case, any defence evidence and legal submissions.
6. The court delivers its judgment. On a conviction, sentencing usually follows later.

## Your position as a defendant

The protections that apply in any criminal trial apply here. You are presumed innocent, the prosecution carries the burden of proof to the criminal standard, you are entitled to disclosure and to legal representation, and you cannot be compelled to give evidence against yourself.

Cases here tend to be document heavy and slow moving, and periods on remand can be long. The work that decides them is done in the disclosure and the legal argument.

## Common questions

### Why is there no jury?

The court hears cases in circumstances where trial by jury is displaced under Irish law. Whether that applies depends on the offence charged or on a certification by the Director.

### Who decides whether I am guilty?

The three judges, sitting together. They decide the facts as well as the law, and they give reasons for the conclusion they reach.

### Can a decision be appealed?

Yes. Appeals lie to the Court of Appeal in the ordinary way, and because the court gives a reasoned judgment an appeal can engage directly with that reasoning. Whether there are grounds needs specific advice.

### Is the hearing public, and is legal aid available?

Hearings are held in public in the ordinary course, though reporting restrictions can apply. Criminal legal aid is available on the same basis as in other criminal courts, subject to means.
