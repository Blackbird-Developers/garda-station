Your rights in a Garda station

These rights apply whether you are guilty or innocent, whether you came in voluntarily or were arrested, and whether or not anyone reminds you of them.

The right to a solicitor of your own choosing

You are entitled to consult a solicitor, and to choose which solicitor. You do not have to accept a name suggested to you, and you are not obliged to continue with a solicitor who has acted for you in the past. If you name us, the member in charge should facilitate contact.

The right to legal advice before questioning

Irish law recognises that legal advice is of little use after the damage is done. If you ask for a solicitor, an interview should not begin until you have had the opportunity to take that advice. If questioning starts anyway, you are entitled to decline to answer until your solicitor has spoken to you.

The right to have a solicitor present during interview

This is more than a phone call beforehand. You are entitled to have your solicitor in the room while you are interviewed, and your solicitor is there to make sure the process is fair.

What a solicitor actually does in the interview room

We advise you before questioning begins on what you are suspected of and what your options are. During the interview we intervene if questions are improper, oppressive or misleading, we make sure breaks and entitlements are respected, and we keep a record. Afterwards we advise you on what has just happened and what is likely to come next.

The right to silence — and its limits

You are not obliged to answer questions put to you. That is a constitutional protection, not a technicality.

However, it is not unlimited. Under certain provisions, in defined circumstances, a court may be permitted to draw an inference from a failure to mention something you later rely on in your defence. Those provisions have conditions attached — including, generally, that you must have had the opportunity to consult a solicitor first.

This is precisely why the decision cannot sensibly be made alone in an interview room. Whether to answer, to answer some questions, or to say nothing depends on what you are suspected of, what the Gardai appear to have, and which legislation you are being held under.

How long you can be held

Detention periods depend entirely on the legislation you are detained under. Different statutes provide for very different maximum periods, some of which can be extended by a senior officer or by a court. Periods of rest, and time when you are receiving medical attention, may not count towards the clock.

We will tell you which provision applies in your case and what it means in practice. Do not assume you know how long you will be there, and be cautious about what you are told informally.

Your treatment while in custody

There are detailed regulations governing the treatment of people in custody in Garda stations. In broad terms you are entitled to:

  • be told why you are being detained, in language you understand;
  • have a nominated person notified of where you are;
  • an interpreter if you need one;
  • medical attention if you need it;
  • reasonable access to food, drink and rest;
  • have your detention and interviews recorded in the custody record.

If you are under 18, additional protections apply and a parent or guardian should normally be present.

The presumption of innocence

It is for the State to prove a case against you. You do not have to prove your innocence, and nothing about being questioned, arrested or charged changes that — however the interview may be made to feel.
